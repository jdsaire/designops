The story
Act 01 · The Problem

Qualified on paper, unfamiliar in the room.

Start with the problem: good work nobody can place.

The reading

I apply for roles whose posts list what a candidate must have. No post lists what a candidate is expected to sound like.

Meeting every requirement is what gets an application considered; being understood is what gets it believed, and only the first is ever written down.

The credential

My own credentials are an MBA, a MicroMasters and a Microsoft specialization. Not one of them showed what I can actually build.

Research estimates work sample predicts job performance at about 3× more than education. A running page shows what I did with the diplomas

The claim

Three in four hiring teams say they receive AI-written applications – turning openings into odysseys for qualified candidates.

65% of hiring managers say skills are harder to verify as anybody can generate a fluent claim in seconds. Every figure here is backed up in public commit history.

The market

Employment in Lima, Peru has grown 22% since 2019, while the share of it held by workers under 25 has fallen by about a third.

Instead of contracting, local market is moving toward work that cannot be automated. That is the direction this website is built toward.

Sources: Sackett et al. (2022) on selection validity; Robert Half, November 2025, n = 2,000+; Deel Global Hiring Report 2026; INEI Encuesta Permanente de Empleo Nacional, May–July 2026.

Act 04 · The Architecture

Two architectures, one standard. The differences are decisions, not accidents.

Some decisions carried across both builds. Others belong to one. Filter to see which.

Show:

Vanilla HTML/CSS/JavaScript

No framework, no build step, no dependency I cannot read end to end.

Nothing to migrate when a major version lands. No security-patch treadmill. A payload small enough to stay fast on a mid-range phone over Peruvian mobile data — which is what most recruiters will use to open it.

Accessibility & responsiveness

WCAG AA held as a build gate, usable with screen readers and keyboards.

Every meaningful image carries alt text, every control is reachable by keyboard with a visible focus state, and contrast is verified in both themes rather than assumed.

Layout resolves at three declared breakpoints — 767px and below, 768 to 1023, and 1024 up. Those are the three the stylesheets actually declare, not a convention borrowed from a framework.

Grid and layout, one page

Ten sections, one type scale, Flexbox and Grid throughout.

The ontology: ten reusable organisms, each owning one job — navigation, hero, work cards, capability carousel, contact.

The taxonomy: design rules declared once at the root as custom properties, inherited everywhere, overridden nowhere.

The choreography: one scroll, one order, every visitor served the same sequence whether or not it was the one they came for.

Orchestration in Claude

Speed came from AI; the standard stayed with me. Workflows orchestrated with Claude and Claude Code, early 2026.

Structured XML dispatches govern every build session — scope, constraints, and approval gates written before execution. Claude Code executes with Plan Mode previews reviewed before code lands; deployment follows hard rules with named commit intent — register, direct push, or a pull request with its link for manual approval — and authorship is logged to a single human author.

Grid and layout, eight pages

The same type scale and token layer, now carried across a shared page chassis.

Each case gets its own route, its own dictionary and its own entry point, so a recruiter can be sent to one page rather than to a scroll position. The cost is a chassis that must stay identical across eight pages — and one defect in it is eight defects.

Orchestration under a scope freeze

The second build runs against a signed, enumerated scope with exit conditions.

Every stage is dispatched as a written prompt with preflight checks, approval gates and a completion report. Improvements that fall outside the list are logged in one line and not built. The discipline is the deliverable as much as the site is.

Act 06 · The Reflection

What worked, what didn't, what I'd keep.

The story reads clean in hindsight. It wasn't. Here is each build, honestly.

House of strawWhat collapsed first

  • Plain-text prompts, no structure.
  • Every session started over.
  • Good intentions, no record.

House of sticksWhat held, but bent

  • Iteration ate whole sessions.
  • One bug outlasted my main environment.
  • Fixes held; the process didn't.

House of bricksWhat stands

  • Versioned dispatches with approval gates.
  • Skills that carry intent between sessions.
  • A written scope that can say no.

The sequel is already dispatched: the Spanish layer, the closing audit, and a second brief series before 2026 ends.

What I'd redoThe migration

  • Moved organisms before deciding where they belonged.
  • Two sections became stubs; one stayed too heavy.
  • Allocation, not architecture, was the mistake.

What bentThe chassis

  • Eight pages share one navigation layer.
  • Two missing tokens broke it on four of them.
  • Static checks passed; the browser didn't.

What standsThe freeze

  • An enumerated scope with exit conditions.
  • Improvements logged in one line, not argued.
  • The project can end, which it previously could not.

The sequel: applications sent, and the parking lot read once — after the fifth one.
