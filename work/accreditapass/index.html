Know before the gate.

AccreditaPass tells a reporter covering the 2026 World Cup what their press pass allows, before a gate turns them away.

Design Research Accessibility Blazor .NET Internationalization
Role — Sole owner Duration — Five weeks · 28 Jul – 30 Aug 2026 Scope — Audit → research → specification → build → re-audit

Picture 104 matches in 16 stadiums across three countries, with every press pass still moving after it is issued. A venue swaps, a zone narrows, a knockout seat waits on a result nobody has played. Without a record, the holder finds out at a turnstile. AccreditaPass keeps one record per journalist that says what changed, why, and what to do next. I audited, researched, specified and built it in five weeks, as a portfolio demonstration rather than a product.

Open the running application Read the repository

An independent portfolio demonstration. Not affiliated with, endorsed by, or connected to FIFA. All accreditation data is simulated for the browser session and nothing is persisted. The match schedule is the one real thing in it.

Simulated research. No human participants took part in any study on this page: every dataset, workshop participant and task-attempt was authored, not recruited.

The story
Act 01 · The Problem

Granted once, changed all tournament.

The holder is the last to know.

The permission

A press pass resolves to venues, zones and fixtures, and any of them can change after it is issued.

Rejection, a mid-tournament reallocation and a denial at the gate are three different events with one experience: the holder learns last, often at the gate itself.

The status page

A page that shows what you hold today answers the wrong question.

A holder needs what changed, when, why and what to do next — a history with reasons, not a snapshot.

The unplayed match

Access to a knockout fixture depends on a result nobody has played yet.

Wording that sounds settled turns a warning into a promise the service cannot keep, so every conditional right is written as a condition.

The app I started with

My own course app returned 26 audit findings, 4 of them critical.

Most traced back to one component that could only be edited, reused on every page. Every finding cites its file and line, and the fix list ran to 19 commits.

Act 02 · The Simulated Pass

No journalist was interviewed. The study says so.

Three passes before the record was built.

Pass 1 · Simulated

Research

A 30-respondent survey and five interviews, both synthetic, produced two personas and a service blueprint.

Pass 2 · Simulated

Ideation

Six authored participants argued 32 ideas down to a shortlist of eight, then to one: Access Record.

Pass 3 · Simulated

Specification

Nine gates fixed the information architecture, WCAG 2.2 AA tokens and the build brief before any interface code.

How might we tell a journalist that their access has changed before they discover it by being refused?

Act 04 · The Build

Built to say what it is.

Blazor on .NET 10, in three languages.

The client

A Blazor WebAssembly client runs on an in-memory mock, so the live site works with no server behind it.

An ASP.NET Core Web API ships in the repository, switched off by default. When it runs, SignalR pushes each new change to the open record.

The languages

English, Spanish and Portuguese carry 339 entries each, at exact parity.

Language and theme are independent of each other and of the session, so changing either never signs you out.

The honesty

The sign-in prints its own passwords, and the API token is a fixed string in the repository.

Neither secures anything, and both say so wherever a reader could mistake them for real. A fixture that has not been played never names its teams, in any language.

The record

Seven routes, and the record is gated because an access record is personal.

Requesting access writes a new entry to your record instead of showing a separate confirmation screen. The schedule and the help page stay public.
