Work brief · 8 min

The World Cup like never before

AccreditaPass tells press teams what their accreditation allows across the tournament, from approval to the final whistle.

Sports Management Media UX Design Front-end Accessibility
Roles — UX researcher · Product designer · Front-end developer Duration — Five weeks · 28 Jul – 30 Aug 2026 Scope — UX research → UX design → prototyping → development
Open the running application Read the repository

104 matches, 16 stadiums and three countries, with no guarantee a press pass is valid for every match. Venues change, spaces shrink and knockout quotas hang on a goal nobody has scored. Without timely notice, journalists run into reality at the stadium gate. AccreditaPass removes that friction: a personal record that shows what changed, why, and the action plan for the best coverage.

Act 01 · The Problem

Access changes. Reporters don't know.

Four failures that turn a valid pass into a wasted trip.

Refusals with no reason

Access withdrawn without a reason breaks the journalist's logistics and leaves no way to respond.

A journalist prepares the kit, plans the coverage and meets a closed gate with nobody behind it. The simulated research rounds found that a refusal with a clear reason is manageable: it can be appealed, worked around or turned into a new angle. A silent one leaves the journalist in the dark. It falls hardest on freelancers and small outlets, who end up relying on corridor talk instead of an official answer.

Late news

Access changes travel slowly and surface when the pass fails outside the stadium.

Teams go out and press positions are reassigned between matchdays: that is the nature of the tournament. The failure is when the paperwork moves slower than the journalist. A reporter pays for a flight, rushes to the stadium and is held twenty minutes before kickoff because gate staff are reading an outdated list. The problem is never the change itself, but the delay in telling the person already standing at the barrier.

The access delusion

Many learn the hard way that an approved accreditation does not guarantee getting through the turnstiles.

It is usually learned at the worst moment: a press credential does not automatically secure entry to the venue. Even veterans who read the fine print get caught by it. One plain line on the confirmation itself would spare the turnstile surprise and save both the day's coverage and the reporter's standing.

Fragile promises

A journalist needs to know the real conditions of knockout-round coverage before buying a flight.

In the knockout rounds everything hangs on the next win. Quarter-final and final positions depend on matches not yet played. When the rules sound settled, a logistical possibility passes for a promise that will break. Stated as conditions from the first day, they would let journalists book flights and hotels on what is likely, not on costly assumptions.

Act 02 · UX Simulation

Smart hypotheses, not imposed designs.

The design research runs that turned doubts into decisions before any code.

Round 1 · Research

Silence breaks trust

A journalist can take bad news; what breaks trust is no explanation and late notice.

Eight stages took the research to a design brief. Simulated survey and interview data, declared as such, and a service blueprint mapped the journalist's journey through tournament coverage. Three problems stood out: institutional silence, late notice, and the belief that a credential equals entry.

Round 2 · Ideation

Real-time tracking

Every change shows what changed, why, and what to do next, delivered before it hurts.

A simulated two-day workshop produced 32 ideas, narrowed through feasibility and prioritisation matrices to a ranked shortlist of eight. The concept that won syncs each update between the journalist and gate staff at once, and scales how much each person hears to how much help they already have.

Round 3 · Prototyping

Architecture decided before code

Flows and screens were locked first, so dev followed a solid structure rather than a guess.

Nine stages turned the concept into a build-ready specification: an information architecture from a simulated card sort, design tokens verified against WCAG 2.2 AA in light and dark, task flows and a data model. It also settled sign-in and support for three languages.

The design challenge

How Might We

How might we soften the impact of an access change on journalists?

Answer

Turn accreditation into a live record: every change reaches the journalist and the venue gate in real time, with its reason and the next step.

Act 04 · The Architecture

One journey, three languages.

The four pillars that hold the build's architecture together.

Front-end decisions

Runs entirely in the browser

The app lives entirely client-side, so a journalist can open it on any phone without depending on a server behind it.

Blazor WebAssembly on .NET 10, written in C#, with a narrow TypeScript layer of two files for theme and locale storage. The app runs on an in-memory provider behind one interface, so every page reads the mock or the API without changing a line. Seven routes; the record is gated because it is personal, while the match schedule and help stay public. English, Spanish and Portuguese run at exact parity, 339 keys each, and switching language or theme never signs you out.

Back-end decisions

Real-time updates

When something changes, the record updates on its own, with no refresh and no support call.

An ASP.NET Core Web API with validated CRUD endpoints and an in-memory store, switched off by default so the live site never depends on it. Its middleware runs in a stated order: CORS, error handling, token authentication, request logging. SignalR carries each change notification to the client. It is a basic layer that proves the contract, not a production back end.

Design system

A structure that scales

Every screen shares one set of design tokens, tuned for a light and a dark theme.

Dual-theme design tokens were defined in the prototyping round and verified against WCAG 2.2 AA in both themes. The dark palette was re-derived against a #000000 anchor rather than inverted. The access card, change row, foreseeable badge and stale indicator share one token set, and appearance follows a three-way setting: light, dark or system.

Accessibility

Inclusive by default

Smooth keyboard navigation and screen-reader support, passing more than twice the accessibility checks it started with.

A new change is announced to assistive technology the moment it happens. Skip links, visible focus at all times and clear labels with input validation are built in. To avoid flooding screen readers, help sections and change rows use progressive disclosure, showing detail only when the user asks for it.
