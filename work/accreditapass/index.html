Never miss a match

AccreditaPass tells reporters covering the 2026 World Cup what their press pass allows, before security turns them away.*

Sports Management Media Relations UX Design Front-end Accessibility
Roles — UX lead · Product designer · Front-end developer Duration — Five weeks · 28 Jul – 30 Aug 2026 Scope — UX research → UX design → prototyping → development → usability testing

Imagine covering up to 104 matches in 16 stadiums across three countries, with every press pass permit still changing after it is issued. A venue swaps, a zone narrows, a knockout seat waits on a result nobody has played. Without a record, the holder finds out at a turnstile. AccreditaPass keeps one record per journalist that says what changed, why, and what to do next. I audited, researched, specified and built it in five weeks.

The story
Act 01 · The Problem

The pass changes. Nobody tells the journalist.

Walk through the four failures that turn a valid pass into a wasted trip.

Refused without a reason

A request stalls or comes back denied with no cause attached, so the journalist cannot tell a fixable gap from a final no.

The research runs found that journalists object less to bad news than to unexplained news. A refusal with a cause is information: it can be appealed, fixed or planned around. A refusal without one is a closed door with nobody behind it. The cost falls hardest on freelancers and small outlets with no press officer to call, who end up learning more from colleagues than from the system that decided.

The news arrives after the damage

Access changes before anyone is told, so the first notice is often a failed scan at the gate.

Quotas are recalculated when a team is eliminated, and positions are reassigned between matchdays, but the change travels slower than its consequence. The cost lands in that gap: a wasted flight, twenty minutes at a barrier before kickoff, gate staff reading a list that is hours out of date. Change itself is expected in the job; the failure is that it never reaches the journalist or the gate in time.

A pass is not a ticket

Accreditation grants a working position, not entry to a match, and many only learn the difference at the barrier.

The split between the media badge and the match ticket is rarely understood from the guidance; it is understood the first time someone is stopped. Even an experienced reporter who read the rules closely can be turned back. Saying it plainly at the moment of approval, on the confirmation itself, costs almost nothing and takes the surprise away from the one place it does the most damage.

The match nobody has played

Access to a knockout match depends on results that do not exist yet, so any promise made today can break tomorrow.

Round-of-32 seats, quarter-final zones and final-day positions all hang on who advances. Wording that sounds settled turns a warning into a promise the service cannot keep. Every conditional right has to read as a condition, naming the result it waits on, so a journalist can book travel around what is likely rather than what was assumed.

Act 02 · UX Simulation

Designed from their side, not ours.

Review the three runs that settled each UX decision before the build.

Run 1 · Research

Silence hurts more than a no

Journalists can live with bad news; what breaks trust is a change with no reason and no warning.

Eight gates took the research from scope to design brief: a research plan, instruments, a simulation note, analysis, artifacts and a limitations file. A 30-respondent survey and five interviews, both synthetic and declared as such, produced two personas, an empathy map, three scenarios and a service blueprint of the accreditation-to-matchday lifecycle. Three themes survived analysis: the silence, the lag, and the badge that is not a ticket.

Run 2 · Ideation

A record, not a status

Every change becomes an entry that says what changed, why, and what to do next, sent before it matters.

A two-day workshop with six authored participants ran divergence twice: 18 risk-sorted assumptions, then 14 more with feasibility suspended, for a pool of 32 ideas. Two prioritization matrices and a Buy a Feature round narrowed it to a ranked shortlist of eight. The concept that survived, Access Record, sends each entry to the journalist and to the venue gate at once, and scales how much each person hears to how much help they already have.

Run 3 · Prototyping

Every screen decided before code

Tasks, screens and the data model were fixed first, so development built against a plan rather than a guess.

Nine gates turned the concept into a buildable specification: competitive benchmarking, an information architecture from a simulated six-participant card sort with ten rules for how the record behaves over time, design tokens verified against WCAG 2.2 AA in light and dark, three task flows, full screen specifications, a data model and a build brief. A four-file addendum later settled the dark palette, sign-in and three-language support.

What the runs asked, and answered

The question

How might we tell a journalist that their access has changed before they discover it by being refused?

The answer: Access Record

Turn accreditation into a live record: every change is written down with its reason and next step, and reaches the journalist and the venue gate before it matters.

Act 04 · The Architecture

From approval to final whistle, in three languages.

Review the four decisions that hold the build together.

Front-end decisions

Runs in your pocket

The whole app lives in the browser, so a journalist can open it on any phone, even with nothing running behind it.

Blazor WebAssembly on .NET 10, written in C#, with a narrow TypeScript layer of two files for theme and locale storage. The app runs on an in-memory provider behind one interface, so every page reads the mock or the API without changing a line. Seven routes; the record is gated because it is personal, while the match schedule and help stay public. English, Spanish and Portuguese run at exact parity, 339 entries each, and switching language or theme never signs you out.

Back-end decisions

News that finds you

When something changes, the record updates on its own, with no refresh and no support call.

An ASP.NET Core Web API with validated CRUD endpoints and an in-memory store, switched off by default so the live site never depends on it. Its middleware runs in a stated order: CORS, error handling, token authentication, request logging. SignalR carries each change notification to the client. It is a basic layer that proves the contract, not a production back end.

Design system

One look, day or night

Every screen shares the same colours, spacing and type, in a light theme and a true-black dark one.

Dual-theme design tokens were defined in the specification run and verified against WCAG 2.2 AA in both themes. The dark palette was re-derived against a #000000 anchor rather than inverted. The access card, change row, foreseeable badge and stale indicator share one token set, and appearance follows a three-way setting: light, dark or system.

Accessibility

Readable by everyone

It works by keyboard and screen reader, with contrast that holds, and passes more than twice the accessibility checks it started with.

A new change is announced to assistive technology the moment it arrives. Every input carries a label, validation errors are announced, focus stays visible and a skip link jumps to the content. Help sections and change rows use two-layer disclosure, so detail never floods a screen reader. A match that has not been played never names its teams, in any language.
