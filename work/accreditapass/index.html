You find out at the gate. This one tells you first.

A journalist's accreditation can change without anyone saying so. I researched, specified and built the record that says so — six documented UX mandates, then a Blazor application, in three languages.

Design Research Design Systems Accessibility Blazor Internationalization

Access changes quietly and gets discovered at a gate. Six documented mandates — audit, research, ideation, specification, evaluation, iteration — converge on one concept: an append-only record where every change carries a reason and a next step. Then I built it.

An independent portfolio demonstration. Not affiliated with, endorsed by, or connected to FIFA. All accreditation data is simulated for the browser session and nothing is persisted. The match schedule is the one real thing in it.

The story
Act 00 · The Context

Accreditation is a permission that keeps moving after it is granted.

A media accreditation is not a ticket. It is a standing permission — which venues, which zones, which fixtures — and it can be narrowed, widened or withdrawn after it has been granted.

The research pass framed the moment precisely: what a journalist needs to be told, and how fast, when their access falls apart after they thought it was settled.

So the question I took was not "how do I show a status." It was: what would a record have to do so that being refused is never how you learn.

The permission moves

Rejection, mid-tournament reallocation, a denial at the gate — three different events, one experience: the holder finds out last.

The status page is the wrong shape

A page that shows what you hold today answers the wrong question. What a holder needs is what changed, when, why, and what to do about it.

Some of it hasn't happened yet

Access to a knockout fixture depends on a result nobody has played. Wording that sounds settled turns a warning into a promise the service cannot keep.

Act 01 · The Problem

I started by auditing the app I already had, and it failed on one thing, twice.

The first pass was a usability and accessibility audit of the app as it stood before any reframing: 26 findings — 4 critical, 12 major, 10 minor — and 12 things it did genuinely well, recorded because an audit that lists only defects misrepresents what was built.

The useful finding was not the total. Six of the ten usability principles failed for the same underlying reason: one component that had only an editable presentation, reused on every page. One structural fix addressed most of them at once.

Every finding cited the file and line it lived at. A claim that could not be pointed at a place in the source was left out rather than stated — which is why the total is 26 and not a rounder, larger number.

A working app and a usable app are not the same thing.

The audit produced a fix list, not a verdict: 26 findings sequenced into 19 commits, each naming the files it touches.

Act 02 · The Research

No real journalist was interviewed. I built the study so a reader can see exactly what that costs.

Simulated research. No human participants took part in any study on this page. Survey and interview datasets, workshop participants and task-attempts were authored, not recruited. Each dossier states in its own scope and limitations files what it can and cannot establish.

Eight gates, in order: scope lock, research plan, instruments, a simulation methodology note, analysis, artifacts, design brief, limitations. Two datasets — a 30-respondent survey and five interviews — both synthetic, both declared as such in their own gate.

It is not empirical evidence about real journalists. It is a documented chain of reasoning from a sourced evidence base to a set of design decisions, each traceable back to its source and labelled by how much weight it can carry.

It produced two personas, an empathy map, three scenarios and a service blueprint of the accreditation-to-matchday lifecycle — and one design question it deliberately left open.

How might we tell a journalist that their access has changed before they discover it by being refused?

Every scope decision is defended in its own file, and each dossier states plainly what it does and does not support. The disclosure is a gate deliverable, not a footnote.

Act 03 · The Concept

Thirty-two ideas, ranked and argued down to one: Access Record.

A second eight-gate mandate turned the open question into a buildable concept. Six participants were authored, not recruited, and every vote, coalition and objection recorded is one person's simulation of a documented method. That is stated at the top of the folder, not buried in it.

Divergence produced 18 risk-sorted assumptions, then 14 more with feasibility suspended — a 32-idea pool. Convergence ran two prioritization matrices and a Buy a Feature round against the whole pool and closed on a ranked shortlist of eight.

The concept that survived: an append-only record where every change carries a reason and a next step, and anything depending on an unplayed result is worded as a condition rather than a decision already taken.

A third mandate — nine gates plus a four-file addendum — turned that concept into a specification: benchmarking, an information architecture from a simulated card sort with ten choreography rules, design tokens verified against WCAG 2.2 AA in both themes, screen states, a data model, and a build brief. No application code was written in it.

Concept, not evidence, and not interface. The folder names the edges where the concept will disappoint before anything was built against it.

Act 04 · The Build

Then I built it, and made the app admit what it is.

A Blazor WebAssembly client in C#, an ASP.NET Core Web API it can read from, SignalR between them, .NET 10 throughout — plus a deliberately narrow TypeScript layer of exactly two files, theme storage and locale storage.

The API is optional and off by default. The app ships with no API configured and runs on an in-memory mock, so the live site works whether or not a server is running anywhere.

Three locales — English, Spanish, Portuguese — at exact parity: 339 entries each, 308 of them interface strings. Language and theme are independent of each other and of the session, so changing either never signs you out.

A fixture that hasn't been played never names its teams, on any screen, in any of the three languages. The schedule the app reads is a record of a completed tournament, and the app is built not to read ahead.

Honest about being fake

The sign-in publishes its own passwords on screen; the API's token is a fixed string printed in the repository. Neither secures anything, and both say so wherever a reader could mistake them for real.

The record, not the status

Seven routes. The record is gated because an access record is personal by definition; the schedule and the help page are public. Requesting access writes a new, animated-in entry to your record rather than showing a separate confirmation screen.

A pipeline with an order

The API's middleware runs in a stated order — CORS, error handling, token authentication, request logging — and the backend documentation set explains why the order is the design, not an accident.

Proof, counted

512 front-end test cases across 36 files and 33 back-end tests, in xUnit and bUnit.

backend/ is documentation; src/backend/ is the code. Two folders, similar names, entirely different contents — the documentation folder is prose that never compiles, deliberately parallel to ux-ui/, which documents the front end's design without containing any of it.

Act 05 · The Evidence

Two measurements, the same instruments, and the movement between them.

Simulated research. No human participants took part in any study on this page. Survey and interview datasets, workshop participants and task-attempts were authored, not recruited. Each dossier states in its own scope and limitations files what it can and cannot establish.

The re-audit had something the first one did not: six task-attempts run against the shipped build rather than derived from source in the abstract. Six of six completed. It is still not real usability testing — no person sat down with this app — and the dossier says so at every gate that matters.

First audit measured the pre-reframing app at commit 0653b4e. Re-audit measured the build at b37066d. Both used simulated task-attempts and authored participants; neither involved a human user.

All 26 findings from the first audit received an explicit disposition — 18 fixed, 5 not applicable, 2 still open, 1 partially fixed. None was silently dropped. Five new findings were raised, and one critical finding accounts for most of what remains.

Two findings were deliberately left out of the remediation sequence rather than resolved, and flagged as such — one needing a demo record that does not exist, one needing a render the dossier could not produce.

A fifth mandate read a separate reference application — ShopEase, my own e-commerce build from the certificate's final front-end course — and realigned this one's navigation, settings, front door, sign-in and match list against it. Six places where that overturned a decision this codebase argues for in its own comments were opened as numbered reversals and closed with the reasoning that resolved them. None was closed by preference alone.

One reversal was closed by being wrong: the premise behind it was factually impossible against the app's own simulated clock. It is recorded that way rather than quietly dropped.

Act 06 · The Reflection

What this build entitles me to say, and what it does not.

This started from the structure of a Blazor course capstone and became my own adaptation — the wider ladder it sits on is told in Brief 01, Front-End Evolution.

I completed Back-End Development with .NET, and its capstone shipped as its own repository.

I do not claim the Full-Stack certificate. I began Full-Stack Integration and set it aside: it needs more dedication than I expected, and those skills would still be forming when this portfolio goes live.

I am learning front-to-back integration. For an experienced full-stack developer that is basic; for a front-end practitioner it is a real gain. I am at that stage and I embrace it.

I came to programming from outside it in higher education, and I learn by building rather than by enrolling.

Future backend capstones, and a second part to this brief series, will tell how that unfolds before 2026 closes.

The dossiers came first and the application came second, and that order is the argument.
