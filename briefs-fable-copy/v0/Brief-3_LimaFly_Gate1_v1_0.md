### 01 — PROBLEM

**H2: Forty million journeys, and the information lives everywhere except one place.**

Role: sole designer — research through prototype. Context: a self-directed build, created ahead of a Lima Airport selection process, to walk into the industry already fluent in its problems. [DERIVED: timing "ahead of the selection process" per public project record; confirm the year you want stated, if any.]

Lima's airport moves 40M+ annual arrivals and departures through a journey that starts before the terminal and ends at the boarding gate. When operations are disrupted — delays, gate changes, congestion — the information a passenger needs scatters across airline apps, screens, announcements, and guesswork. Stress rises exactly where clarity should be cheapest.

And there is a second, quieter problem: the hours passengers spend waiting are commercially valuable and largely unaddressed — attention with nowhere useful to go.

LimaFly was built to answer both in one product: centralize the journey's information to soften disruptions, and give the waiting time a commercial layer worth engaging with.

### 02 — INTERVENTION

**H2: I mapped the whole journey, then built the system before the screens.**

The prototype covers the complete passenger journey — pre-experience at home, arrival, security, dwell, and through to the boarding gate — designed as one continuous product rather than a set of disconnected features.

Two layers structure it. The core is an information-centralization hub: flight status, wayfinding, and disruption alerts in one place, designed to mitigate operational disruptions across the journey. On top of it sits a gamified commercial layer, giving dwell time a reason to convert — engagement mechanics connected to the airport's commercial offer. [INVENTED-verificar: the specific gamification mechanics (points, missions, rewards) — confirm what the prototype actually contains so this line can name it precisely.]

The build was system-first, which is the decision I'd defend hardest. Before high-fidelity screens existed, the foundation did: color, typography, and iconography defined as local variables (tokens), components built with variants, and layouts on Auto Layout. Every screen after that was assembled from governed parts — so consistency wasn't a review step, it was the default, and iterating the journey didn't mean redrawing it.

### 04 — OUTCOME

**Metric chip:** Complete journey — prototyped

Prose: A high-fidelity, working prototype of the complete passenger journey, built on a scalable token-and-component foundation — and presented as a working artifact in a Lima Airport selection process. It did what it was built to do: demonstrate industry fluency with a product, not a claim. ### 05 — RETROSPECTIVE

**H2: The prototype earned its next life as software.**

Two lessons held. First, prototyping an industry before entering it works: the fluency LimaFly built showed up in the work I later did inside the airport. Second, system-first pays off at exactly the moment you need it — every iteration during the build cost hours, not days, because the foundation absorbed the change.

The honest next step is already underway: LimaFly is being rebuilt as a functional application — the same journey and system thinking, carried from prototype into working code. That rebuild is an active project, not a promise, and it is the natural continuation of the discipline documented in the DesignOps System brief: foundations first, then the product on top.
