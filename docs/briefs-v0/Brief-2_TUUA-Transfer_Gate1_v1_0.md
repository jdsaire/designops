### 01 — PROBLEM

**H2: A fee with no precedent needed a product with no playbook.**

Role: Innovation Specialist, Lima Airport Partners. Duration: May–October 2025, on the airport's most critical program after the new terminal opening. My contribution: define the digital payment product end to end and articulate the program's planning phase across the central divisions it touched.

After the new terminal opened, Lima Airport had to charge an airport usage fee (TUUA) to international-transfer passengers — a fee this airport had never collected from this segment, through a channel that did not exist. There was no legacy interface to iterate on and no internal precedent to copy. What did exist: five central areas — Finance, IT, Terminals, Commercial, and Corporate Reputation — each with its own requirements, and an engineering team that needed a fully defined product, not a concept, to build against.

Two failure modes threatened the program: a payment flow that lost passengers mid-journey, and a planning phase where five divisions' specifications never converged into one buildable standard. My work sat exactly on those two risks.

### 02 — INTERVENTION

**H2: I defined the product end to end, then made the handoff survivable.**

The product work ran the full arc: I designed the payment experience, prototyped it, and evaluated the flows before anything reached engineering. The passenger case is unforgiving — travelers in transit, often mid-connection, paying an unfamiliar fee on their own devices — so the flow was designed to be completed quickly and understood immediately, and the prototype was evaluated against that bar rather than against internal taste. [INVENTED-verificar: evaluation method and iteration specifics — confirm how you tested (heuristic evaluation, user walkthroughs, stakeholder reviews) so this sentence can be grounded.]

The handoff was engineered as a deliverable in its own right: the complete user flow and mockups, delivered to engineering as the build specification. Pilot-to-production discipline means the defining team's output must stand on its own once the building team takes over — so the specification, not standing meetings, carried the product intent.

In parallel, I articulated the program's planning phase across the five central areas, aligning deliverables, specifications, and timelines into a single standard. Finance, IT, Terminals, Commercial, and Corporate Reputation each held a piece of the program; the planning work made those pieces converge instead of collide.

### 05 — RETROSPECTIVE

**H2: A handoff is a product too.**

The lesson I carry from this program: the quality of a handoff is measurable in how little the receiving team needs you afterward. Designing the flow was half the work; making the specification complete enough for engineering to build without reinterpretation was the other half — and it's the half most design work skips.

The same applies across divisions. Five central areas didn't align because of goodwill; they aligned because deliverables, specifications, and timelines were written into one standard everyone could execute against. That is the operating discipline I'd bring to a design organization: products defined so they ship, and coordination written down so it scales.
